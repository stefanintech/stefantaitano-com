I'm finally back from RubyConf 2026 in Las Vegas. It was my first Ruby conference, my first Ruby-related tech event, and only my second tech conference ever.

Toward the end of it, I had this surprisingly emotional feeling that I did not want the conference to end. I thought that was pretty amazing.

I went because I wanted to get involved with the Ruby community. At the beginning of the year, I wrote that [I was learning Ruby on Rails](/articles/it-is-2026-and-im-learning-ruby-on-rails/). Only a couple of months ago, I finally made it onto the Ruby on Rails path in The Odin Project.

I knew a little bit of Ruby going into the conference. Mostly syntax, honestly. Enough to recognize what I was looking at, but not enough to walk into a room full of Ruby developers and feel like I knew what I was doing.

That can be intimidating. Believe it or not, though, a few months into learning Ruby was still enough time for me to volunteer and help out. A lot of it was showing up at the right place, at the right time, and helping wherever I was needed.

On top of that, I got picked to give my first lightning talk. For someone only a couple of months into this, that still feels pretty rare and pretty cool. I even got to close out the night with it.

The talk was not about a clever Ruby technique because I definitely was not the person to give that talk. It was about volunteering before I felt ready, which was the most honest thing I could talk about.

One of my slides just says, "Nobody asked." Nobody asked me to become a Ruby developer. Nobody handed me a checklist that ended with permission to participate. At some point I had to stop waiting for a future version of myself to feel qualified enough to show up.

The human part of the conference is what I keep thinking about most. I met some really cool people, but I was not trying to leave with 50 or 60 new connections. I would rather get to know a handful of people beyond the surface level.

What do you like doing outside of work? What are you building? What are you looking forward to? What have the ups and downs of life looked like for you?

We are all human. Getting to have those deeper conversations was probably the best part of the whole week.

I also got to meet the maintainers and developers behind RubyEvents, an open-source project I had wanted to contribute to before I even arrived. I prepared ahead of time by reaching out to one of the maintainers who streams on Twitch and spending some time understanding the codebase. I wanted to show up with an idea of what I was doing instead of opening the repository for the first time at the conference.

At the hack space, I was able to work through the contribution with the people who actually maintain the project. [My first Ruby-related pull request was merged that day](https://github.com/rubyevents/rubyevents/pull/1940), updating RubyConf 2026 involvement and speaker information.

Cheers to open source and to awesome maintainers who make it feel possible to contribute.

I also learned about Git's patch mode while working through the changes. I already forgot the exact combination of flags, which feels about right, but I remember thinking, "Wow, I need to use this every single time."

Outside of the conference, I got to visit my sister and her boyfriend, explore a little bit of Vegas with family, eat some casino floor food, and spend time around Summerlin, somewhere I had never been before. I did not do any gambling. I did get to watch the sunset over the mountains on the west side, which was pretty awesome.

All of that made the trip feel like more than just a conference.

The point I wanted to leave behind in the talk was simple:

> Participation is not a reward for expertise.

Community is not only what you join after you are ready. Sometimes it is how you get ready.

RubyConf did not magically turn me into a Ruby developer. I am still working through The Odin Project. I still know how much I do not know. The difference is that I no longer think learning, building, and participating have to happen in that exact order.

I already want to volunteer again. Whether the next chance is RailsCamp, RubyConf, or another Ruby event, I am in. The first one was pretty awesome, so why not?
